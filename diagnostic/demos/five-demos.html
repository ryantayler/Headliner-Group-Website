You are cash flow constrained

You're profitable on paper and short of cash in practice. The work is sold and the money hasn't landed, so there's nothing sitting there to fund the next job. You get paid with nothing upfront and the balance lands 52 days later. You're owed more than a month of revenue past its due date and you've been delaying payments most months. You're holding two to four weeks of cover.

That's a timing problem rather than a rate problem. Every job you win right now costs you money before it pays you, which is why winning more of them is currently the worst move available to you.

How to fix it

The job is to shorten the gap between doing the work and getting paid for it. Nothing else moves until that does.

  1. You take nothing upfront, which is this whole problem in one answer. Change that before anything else on this list.
  2. Take a deposit on every job. Fifty percent thirty days out and the balance the week before, so the money is in before your costs go out.
  3. Line the deposit up to land before your cost of goods does. On jobs where the number moves on the day, charge the balance afterwards and refund the difference.
  4. Invoice within twenty four hours of the job finishing, rather than at the end of the month.
  5. Collect what's already overdue before you chase anything new. It's the cheapest money available to you.

You are talent constrained

Talent here means a missing layer, not missing hands. Four of the five layers sit with you rather than with somebody whose job it is. Most of last week went to quoting and pricing jobs. The work that actually moves the business forward got none of it and if you went dark for a month the business stops without you. Things stall for want of anyone to hand them to as a matter of routine.

Hiring another pair of hands underneath you doesn't touch this. What's missing is a whole role that doesn't exist yet, and until it does, everything you add lands on the same desk.

How to fix it

One role, hired with the authority to actually run what you hand over.

  1. List every task you touched last week. Against each one, write the name of the person who'd do it if you were away for a month. Any task with no name against it is the hire.
  2. Hire the layer rather than the hands. Another delivery person gives you more capacity. A person who runs delivery gives you your week back.
  3. You've got more direct reports than one person can run properly. The hire that fixes this sits between you and most of them.
  4. Hand over one whole area with the decisions attached. Half a handover leaves you doing the work and them carrying the confusion.
  5. Give it ninety days before you judge it, and stop being the escalation point for what you handed over.

You are fulfilment constrained

Nothing at the front of the business needs attention until the back of it can breathe. Anything you do to bring in more work right now gets paid for by the customers you already have.

How to fix it

More output from what you already have, before you buy any more of anything.

  1. Find the one step everything queues behind, then put your next hour into that step and nothing else.

  3. Pull the work that doesn't need your best people off your best people.
  4. Quote the lead time you actually deliver rather than the one you hope for. A blown promise costs a customer you'd already won.
  5. Stop selling the jobs that eat the most capacity for the least return, at least until the queue clears.

You are offer constrained

Enquiries arrive and they don't convert. Your close rate sits at 9% and the most common reason you lose is inaction. Prospects come back on your quote asking what's included on most quotes and they find out what you charge from a quote built from scratch every time.

This isn't a sales technique problem. When the thing on the table is clearly priced and it fits what the buyer came in for, an average salesperson closes it. Right now yours needs you in the room to make sense of it.

How to fix it

Make the thing you sell understandable without you standing next to it.

  1. Write the offer on one page. What it is, what it costs, what the buyer gets, who it isn't for.
  2. Price it publicly, or at least in a range. Every quote you build from scratch is a delay you're funding.
  3. Sort your last twenty losses by reason and count them. The pattern is usually one thing.
  4. Rewrite the quote so it answers what's included and why it costs that, before they have to ask.
  5. Cut the options. Fewer, clearer choices close more often than a menu does.

You are margin constrained

You're full and busy, and there's nothing left at the end of it. What's left after direct costs sits at 11% and after everything it's 0%. You've never raised your prices and jobs run over what you quoted on most jobs.

Volume won't rescue this. At the margin you're running, more work gets you more of the same result and it arrives faster.

How to fix it

Price first, then cost. Price moves faster and costs nothing to change.

  1. Raise prices on new work now. Ten percent across the board, and watch what actually happens.
  2. Work out what each job really costs, your own time included, then rank your last twenty by what was left.
  3. Quote a contingency into the jobs that historically run over. You already know which ones they are.
  4. Find your worst three customers by margin, then reprice them or let them go.
  5. Stop discounting to win. A discount is the fastest way to buy work you'll resent delivering.
