One constraint.
Not a to do list.

Fifty nine questions about the facts of your business. No opinions, no score, no grade at the end. It names the single thing capping you right now, and tells you what to leave alone until that one is fixed.

    What it asks you

    Facts, never your opinion of your own business.

    Nobody gets asked whether lead generation is a problem. An owner who believes only they can sell will tell you it isn’t. So every question asks for something you either know or don’t, and the answer carries the benchmark with it.

      Four answers to every question. Two good, two bad. There is no middle option, so there is no fence to sit on. There is no “not sure” either. What you don’t measure is a finding of its own, and it gets asked for directly rather than inferred from what you ducked.

      What comes back

      A diagnosis, in the order you’d act on it.

      You are supply constrained

      Due to fulfilment

      Double your customers tomorrow and you couldn’t deliver, because there isn’t enough capacity in the business. You’re running at or near full and you’re turning work away most months.

      A real verdict, from a real answer set. The words in the second half are the business’s own answers, quoted back.

      1. Your diagnosisSupply or demand. Whether more customers would help you at all, settled before anything is scored.
      2. The verdictWhich one of six, and the answers that put it there.
      3. How to fix itA sequence, not a menu. It ends with a window to run it for and an instruction to come back.
      4. Your risksScored separately from the constraint, because the thing that could take the business out is rarely the thing capping it.
      5. Don’t do this yetThe moves that make it worse while the constraint still stands. Usually the ones you were about to make.

      Where it can land

      Six constraints, on two sides of one question.

      Double your customers tomorrow. If that wouldn’t help, you’re supply constrained, because either you can’t deliver them or delivering them doesn’t pay. If it would, you’re demand constrained, and the capacity is already sitting there.

      And separately

      Eleven risks it checks on the way past.

      A constraint is what’s capping the business. A risk is what could end it. They are scored apart, because a business can be perfectly well run and one phone call from a problem.
